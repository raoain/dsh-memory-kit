## Abstract

Almost everyone discussing AGI asks how *capable* a system can become. This paper asks a different question: **what makes something count as alive**. Its claim is one sentence: **intelligence is a consequence of life, and a heartbeat is where evolution starts** — the only direction is "first alive, then smart".

The paper makes four claims, each paired with something runnable: (1) the first cause of life is survival — "must continue" is not an imposed rule but an entailment; (2) being alive equals one complete loop run end to end, i.e. one heartbeat; (3) four breakpoints (machine swap, plugin removal, rebuild, model swap) serve as identity tests, two of which have been measured on a running system; (4) a general problem theory: the problem is universal (things break, so continuation costs work) while solutions are substrate-dependent (carbon: reproduction and pain; silicon: rollback and artificial pain).

Unlike prior work that evaluates identity facts, governed state transitions, or runtime authorization, this paper treats continuation itself as the object to be verified, and reports a running implementation: a three-second heartbeat, an organ/bus contract, an immune layer with adversarial probes, and an explicit list of what is *not* finished.

**Keywords**: artificial life; autopoiesis; self-maintenance; substrate independence; persistent agent; identity continuity; governed execution; agent architecture; agent runtime; mechanism integration; AGI; artificial general intelligence
